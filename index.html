1 · Twelve notes

Western music uses 12 notes that repeat in every octave: C, C♯, D, D♯, E, F, F♯, G, G♯, A, A♯, B, then C again, twice as high. The step from one to the next is a semitone (one piano key, black or white).

A note between two letters has two names: C♯ (“C sharp”, German Cis) is the same key as D♭ (“D flat”, German Des).

German trap: English B is German H, and English B♭ is German B. DJ software uses English names, so “Bm” means h-Moll, not b-Moll.

2 · Keys: 7 of the 12 notes

A key is a set of 7 notes that a song mostly uses, plus a “home” note it keeps returning to (the root). There are 12 possible roots and two common flavours, so 24 keys:

  • Major (Dur): sounds bright, happy, stable. On the Camelot wheel: B.
  • Minor (Moll): sounds darker, sadder, more tense. On the wheel: A.

3 · Why the wheel works

The Camelot wheel is the circle of fifths (Quintenzirkel) with simple codes. Each step around the wheel moves the root a fifth (7 semitones) and changes exactly one of the 7 notes. That is why neighbours sound good together: they share 6 of 7 notes.

  • Number 1–12: position on the wheel, like hours on a clock.
  • Letter: A = minor (inner ring), B = major (outer ring).
  • Same number, other letter: the relative key (Paralleltonart). 8A (A minor) and 8B (C major) use exactly the same 7 notes, only the home note differs.

4 · Mixing moves

Starting from a track in 8A, these are the classic choices for the next track:

MoveExampleShared notesFeels like
Same key8A → 8A7/7Seamless
One step (±1)8A → 7A / 9A6/7Smooth, the standard move
Relative (A↔B)8A → 8B7/7Mood flips darker ↔ brighter
Energy boost (+2)8A → 10A5/7Noticeable lift, keep it short
Semitone up (+7)8A → 3A2/7Hard key change, not for long blends
Clash8A → 2A2/7Sour – avoid blending

“Blend” plays track 1 alone, then both tracks together, like when two decks overlap.

5 · Three ways to write a key

Rekordbox and Serato can show Camelot or classic notation (e.g. C#m); Traktor uses Open Key, where d = major, m = minor and the numbers are shifted by 7 (8B = 1d).

6 · In practice

  • Key detection software is often wrong, especially with techno, drums-only intros or tracks without a clear melody. Trust your ears over the label.
  • Key matters most during long blends where melodies overlap. Quick cuts or drum-only transitions hide clashes.
  • Tempo changes key: speeding a track up with the pitch fader also raises its key unless key lock / master tempo is on.
